LONG-DURA TION SCREEN VIEWING

ABSTRACT
Appearance-based gaze estimation offers a low-cost alternative to
infrared eye tracking for screen-based behavioral and clinical ap-
plications, however existing models are typically developed for
wide ranges of gaze angle and head pose. Prolonged screen view-
ing presents a distinct regime in which gaze remains near the screen
center, head motion is limited, and calibration drift accumulates over
time. In this work, we benchmark six published estimators along
with a proposed differential-gaze model on 140 long-duration facial
video recordings with synchronized eye tracking under subject-
disjoint evaluation and a common budget of calibration frames.
Differential estimation was the only static method that significantly
improved upon a baseline predictor of each recording’s mean gaze,
and was further boosted by addition of temporal context. It also
yielded the strongest agreement with reference fixations and sac-
cades, demonstrating that low angular error alone could not validate
eye movement reconstruction. These findings establish differential
estimation as a promising foundation for reliable gaze tracking in
long-duration, narrow-range settings.
Index Terms—Gaze Estimation, Eye Tracking, Differential
Regression, Subject-specific Calibration, Temporal Context
1. INTRODUCTION
Appearance-based gaze estimation, in which eye movements are in-
ferred from camera recordings of the face, has been adopted as a low-
cost and unobtrusive alternative to infrared eye tracking for screen-
based behavioral and clinical analysis [1, 2], such as for example in
studies of reading [3] and visual attention [4]. The most effective
current systems are based on convolutional and transformer models
trained on curated datasets that span wide ranges of head pose and
gaze angle [5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. Model accuracy for this task is typically
reported as the mean angular error over those ranges.
Many clinical studies that would benefit from appearance-based
gaze estimation involve prolonged screen viewing [3, 4], where the
gaze range is narrow and concentrated near the screen center, the
head is near-stationary, and the sessions extend long enough for the
eye-tracker calibration to drift [10]. Such conditions are largely
absent from the data on which current gaze estimation models are
trained [5, 6], and hence their robustness in low-resource settings
has been little examined. Moreover, the constant predictor, i.e., the
per-recording mean gaze, is already accurate in narrow ranges, so a
low mean angular error is not necessarily evidence of model robust-
ness [11]. As a result, more descriptive metrics should be evaluated.

In this study we investigate this regime as a case study on paired
data of synchronized facial video and high-fidelity eye tracking as
ground truth, from a long-duration screen-viewing task. Published
appearance-based gaze models, combined with a lightweight subject
adaptation, were evaluated under a common subject-independent
protocol, together with behaviorally-grounded metrics defined at the
level of fixations and saccades. A custom model based on differen-
tial gaze estimation was the only frame-wise system to significantly
outperform the constant predictor, while significantly improving
gaze metrics. Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We introduce a benchmark for appearance-based gaze esti-
mation under narrow-range screen viewing, built on synchro-
nized facial video and high-fidelity eye tracking.
• We also contribute a suite of behaviorally-grounded evalu-
ation metrics including per-recording fixation and saccade-
level angle agreement to complement angular error.
• We show that published models, evaluated under a subject-
independent protocol, barely exceed the per-recording con-
stant predictor after anchor-based calibration.
• We demonstrate that a model trained on differential gaze es-
timation and conditioned on temporal context yields substan-
tial improvements in gaze metrics of narrow-range viewing.
2. DA TASET
Our gaze estimation benchmark uses paired facial video and high-
fidelity eye tracking recordings from the PRECOG project [10, 12],
a multimodal study investigating the neural and cognitive mecha-
nisms underlying depression and suicidal ideation. The original data
collection included 146 college-aged adults who completed two cog-
nitive tasks while neural, physiological and behavioral signals were
recorded [10]. For the purpose of this study we use facial video
acquired at 195 Hz with a monochrome Teledyne FLIR Blackfly S
camera and gaze data acquired at 250 Hz with an EyeLink 1000 Plus
tracker. The resulting dataset contains an average of 75 minutes of
paired recordings per participant, totaling 190 hours.
Both tasks were administered using the same camera configura-
tion and a 1920×1080 display measuring 510×286 mm, viewed
from a distance of 862.7 mm and subtending 33° horizontally and
18.8° vertically. Participants completed either a fast-paced Stroop
task comprising 480 trials interleaved with brief rest periods or a
longer sentence-processing task interleaved with free-viewing in-
tervals [10]. Despite differing in cognitive demand, both tasks ex-
hibit the challenging viewing regime considered here: gaze remained
predominantly near the screen center, head movement was limited,
and sessions lasted long enough for participant pose and eye-tracker
calibration to potentially drift. Annotations from the EyeLink eye
tracker were additionally used as reference intervals for model esti-
mates of fixations, saccades, and blinks.

3. METHODS
3.1. Benchmark Models
To evaluate appearance-based gaze estimation in our setting we
benchmarked six published model configurations spanning full-face
and eye-region inputs, convolutional and transformer architectures,
and static and temporal prediction. Each model was retrained on the
common PRECOG dataset while retaining its native input represen-
tation, gaze parameterization, and training procedure. Predictions
were converted to a common yaw–pitch representation before cali-
bration and evaluation. We additionally implemented two matched
custom systems: a custom CNN trained to regress absolute gaze and
a differential estimator trained to predict gaze changes.
Full-face 448:The model of Zhang et al. [8] directly regresses
gaze from a normalized448×448facial image. Its AlexNet-based
architecture applies a learned spatial weighting map to the final con-
volutional features, allowing regions informative of eye and head
orientation to contribute selectively to the prediction.
RT-GENE[7] estimates gaze from normalized crops of the left
and right eyes together with head pose. The two eye streams are
encoded separately before being combined with the pose represen-
tation for angular regression, making the contributions of ocular ap-
pearance and head orientation explicit.
ETH-XGaze.We use the baseline model [6], which applies a
residual convolutional network to a normalized full-face image and
predicts gaze pitch and yaw. ETH-XGaze was developed for large
variations in camera viewpoint, head pose, and gaze direction, pro-
viding a useful contrast to the regime considered here.
GazeTR[9] combines convolutional feature extraction with
transformer-based self-attention. Its hybrid variant first produces a
spatial feature map using ResNet-18 and models global relationships
with a transformer encoder before regressing gaze direction.
Gaze360[5] was developed for unconstrained gaze estimation
across wide variations in pose, viewing distance, and context. A
ResNet-18 encodes a normalized head crop, and the temporal model
aggregates consecutive embeddings using a bidirectional LSTM. We
evaluate both the single-frame backbone and its temporal model to
assess the contribution of temporal context.
Direct CNN:As a baseline control, we trained an ImageNet [13]-
initialized ResNet-18 [14] on the same binocular crops used by our
differential model. The network encodes each crop independently
and directly predicts absolute yaw and pitch from a single frame.
The differential gaze estimator is built on top of this baseline.
3.2. Differential Gaze Estimator
Our custom model of gaze estimation is motivated by the fact that the
mapping from eye appearance to gaze direction contains participant-
and session-specific terms, including eye shape, camera geometry,
and seating position [15]. All previous models must learn these nui-
sance factors together with the eye movements of interest. Differen-
tial estimation instead predicts the change between two observations,
allowing additive recording-specific offsets to cancel from the train-
ing target [16]. We adapt that formulation to our setting by encoding
two random crops from the same recording with a weight-shared
Direct CNN. A two-layer head maps the difference between their
embeddings,e i ande j, to gaze displacement:

The head has no bias, ensuringh(0) = 0. At inference, the absolute
gaze for queryqis reconstructed relative to a labeled anchora:

and predictions are averaged across all anchors. Distributing anchors
throughout a recording limits the one-sided temporal extrapolation
associated with calibration performed only at session onset. For a
temporal variant, we replaced the single crop with a window of 5
frames spanning either 3.2 or 6.4 s, whose embeddings were con-
textualized by a one-layer bidirectional GRU [17] with 128 hidden
units; its center step was then passed to the differential head.
4. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
4.1. Pre-processing
Pairing the two modalities required a temporal alignment estimated
per recording, since the camera and the eye tracker operated on in-
dependent clocks. Linear drift from the camera clock was estimated
relative to the nominal 195 fps rate and then removed, whereas the
residual offset was quantified and corrected through blink alignment,
allowing a maximum measured discrepancy of 31 ms. Thirteen par-
ticipants were excluded because of unreliable EyeLink recordings
and 33 because their video and gaze signals could not be reliably
aligned. The finalized dataset comprised 140 recordings from 100
participants, totaling approximately 115 hours, or 80.4 million video
frames, with an average of 70 minutes per participant.
Facial landmarks were detected using MediaPipe [18] and used
to extract the face, head, and eye-region inputs required by eachmodel. The differential and Direct CNN systems received80×224
binocular eye crops, while the published models retained their native
input regions and resolutions. Where required by the pipeline, inputs
were mapped to a normalized camera space using a perspective-
warping procedure [19] that standardizes camera distance, scale,
and in-plane rotation. EyeLink screen coordinates were projected
into three-dimensional camera coordinates using the known display
geometry and viewing distance, then expressed as yaw and pitch.
Model outputs represented as three-dimensional gaze vectors were
converted to the same angular convention.
For evaluation, the 195 Hz video was first decimated to a com-
mon 65 Hz clip index and then sampled at 5 Hz for analysis. Frames
occurring during EyeLink-annotated blinks were excluded from cal-
ibration and evaluation but retained during training. The 100 partic-
ipants were divided into five subject-disjoint folds, with three folds
used for training, one for validation, and one for testing in each rota-
tion. After excluding recordings with fewer than 250 eligible evalu-
ation samples, the common test set comprised 144,743 samples from
105 recordings and 76 participants in total.
4.2. Training Protocol
The differential encoder was initialized from an ImageNet-pretrained
ResNet-18 and produced a 256-dimensional embedding. It was
trained for 4 epochs from a single seed using AdamW [20], with
a batch size of 256, learning rate of8×10 −4, weight decay 0.01,
and dropout 0.2. The learning rate followed two epochs of linear
warm-up and subsequent cosine decay. A smooth-L 1 displacement
loss was supplemented by a unit-weighted amplitude penalty. The
published models were retrained on the same subject partitions using
their respective training objectives and implementation procedures.
The temporal arms followed the same recipe with the batch reduced
to 96 due to memory capacity; thematchedstatic arm of Table 2
was retrained under that same schedule to avoid confounds.
4.3. Test-time Calibration
Every model received a common set of 20 labeled calibration frames
per recording, distributed approximately uniformly across the valid
frames of the session. Each query was required to lie at least 325
frames in the 65 Hz clip, corresponding nominally to 5 s, from ev-
ery anchor. Because anchors were distributed across the full record-
ing and could occur later than the reconstructed query, our primary
protocol targets offline reconstruction, or online gaze tracking with
assumed distributed calibration phases. For each axis, we fitted an
affine correction and clipped its least-squares slope to[0.5,2.0]to
prevent unstable scaling from anchors spanning a narrow gaze range.
For the differential model, each anchor was reconstructed us-
ing only the other 19 anchors before the affine correction was fitted,
because allowing an anchor to reconstruct itself would leak its la-
bel into its own prediction. The resulting 20 leave-one-anchor-out
(LOO) predictions were paired with their labels to fit the per-axis
correction. The published models required no such exclusion be-
cause their raw anchor predictions were independent of the labels.
4.4. Evaluation protocol
Frame-level accuracy was measured as the great-circle angle be-
tween the predicted and reference gaze directions. We additionally
report within-recording Pearson correlations for yaw and pitch. An-
gular error was evaluated relative to a label-informed constant pre-
dictor, i.e., the mean reference gaze of each recording. To assess
whether a model follows eye movements rather than remaining near
the center of the observed gaze distribution, we also evaluated fine-
grained fixation- and saccade-level agreement, where the respective

5. RESULTS & DISCUSSION
5.1. Performance under narrow-range viewing

20-anchor protocol. The published estimators remain close to the
label-informed recording mean (1.780°) with error ratios between
0.995 and 1.019, despite exhibiting moderate within-recording cor-
relations with the ground-truth gaze. This effect illustrates the lim-
itation of angular error metrics in evaluating fine-grained gaze es-
timation in narrow viewing settings. The differential estimator is
the only static one to exceed this baseline, reducing angular error

at 0.636 and 0.447, respectively. In paired participant-level com-
parisons, its angular error is lower and its yaw correlation higher
than those of every published comparator after FDR correction (all
p <0.001). Its improvement over the constant predictor is also sig-

5.3. Alignment to event annotations
Mean angular error can measure absolute proximity to the reference
gaze but does not directly establish whether a model followed eye
movements or regressed to the session mean. We therefore eval-
uated video grounding at the level of EyeLink-annotated fixations
and saccades. The event-level analysis retained all recordings that
contained at least 20 eligible saccades, yielding 69,754 fixations and
15,111 saccades of at least 1°. Figure 2 illustrates the (static) dif-
ferential model’s predictions for fixation position, saccade ampli-
tude, and saccade direction. As we show in Table 4, the published
models differ more significantly on movement-level measures than
on frame-level angular error. Gaze360-LSTM is the strongest pub-
lished comparator, reaching fixation correlations of 0.629 in yaw and
0.533 in pitch, a saccade-amplitude correlation of 0.522, and a me-
dian directional error of 35.5°. The single-frame differential model
improves these values to 0.715, 0.548, 0.597, and 31.2°, respectively.
With 6.4 s of temporal context, it further reduces directional error to
30.7°. Paired comparisons on both saccade endpoints remain signif-
icant against all published configurations (all correctedp <0.01).
Frame-level and movement-level evaluation therefore capture
different aspects of model sensitivity. Across the published config-
urations, the rankings produced by angular error and saccade agree-
ment were only weakly aligned. GazeTR, for example, had the
largest frame-level angular error but substantially better directional
agreement than that error would suggest for other backbones. Re-
porting movement-level agreement alongside angular error therefore
distinguishes models that track the eye from those that merely drift
to the center of its observed range.
6. CONCLUSION
We evaluated appearance-based gaze estimation under narrow-range
screen viewing using a subject-disjoint protocol. Published models
remained close to a recording-mean predictor, whereas differential
estimation achieved the lowest angular error and strongest fixation-
and saccade-level agreement, with further gains from provided tem-
poral context. Relative to the uncalibrated direct regressor, the com-
plete differential pipeline reduced error by 46% in total. These find-
ings support differential estimation for reliable gaze reconstruction
in constrained viewing settings. Generalization remains nevertheless
limited by the college-aged cohort of our dataset, the single camera
geometry of our setup, and the offline calibration.7. COMPLIANCE WITH ETHICAL STANDARDS

in [10]. No new data were collected for this work.
